== [0]/summary
The literature agrees that when the verifier is reliable, coverage keeps rising with the number of samples, roughly log-linearly (Large Language Monkeys). It also agrees that i.i.d. best-of-N is the least compute-efficient way to buy coverage at small budgets. Step-level search guided by a prior does better. Verifier beam search matches best-of-16 at N=4 (4x) and best-of-256 at N=16 (Hugging Face test-time compute blog), and compute-optimal allocation is more than 4x more efficient than best-of-N (Snell et al.). Sampling without replacement at the root is what makes 2–16-simulation search budgets work (Kool et al. 2019; Gumbel AlphaZero/MuZero). Our own diagnostics fit the regime where policy-guided search beats both i.i.d. sampling and uniform BFS. Per-step optimality is 87% but open-loop success is 24.5%, 51 of 76 failures are wrong at the first action, and there are at most 5 actions per step, so errors compound across sampled plans. LevinTS formalises this: it needs at most d/π(path) expansions, so a decent per-step prior costs far fewer world-model calls than BFS-256. When no candidate reaches the goal (76.5% of episodes), our rerank falls back to the first candidate. The standard fix is a learned value or goal-distance used for ranking and as a search heuristic (POLO, PHS, process reward models, WL-GOOSE). Expert iteration and search distillation reliably amortize search: ExIt, ReST-EM, STaR, Stream of Search (25.7%→51.3%), Searchformer, Dualformer (fast mode 80% vs 30% for solution-only), SiGPlan, and a 270M chess model at Lichess blitz Elo 2895 without search. The best quantitative anchor is Jones (2021): each 10x of train compute removes about 15x of test-time compute. The catch is that expert iteration and RL usually raise pass@1 while narrowing pass@k (Havrilla 2024; Yue 2025). A K-candidate system therefore needs diversity-preserving targets, and best-of-N-aware fine-tuning lifted Gemma-2B pass@32 from 60.0% to 67.0%. Small-model test-time scaling claims (a 1B model matching 8B and a 3B model beating 70B on MATH-500 with search; 0.5B beating GPT-4o) depend on strong verifiers and careful compute counting. On Blocksworld, RAP with LLaMA-33B beat GPT-4 chain-of-thought (6-step 0.42 vs 0.40), while a fine-tuned GPT-3 alone reached only 20% on PlanBench. A fair amortization claim must count proposer tokens as well as world-model calls in one FLOPs currency, and compare success-vs-compute curves against uniform-policy search and BFS at matched compute, plus training compute and break-even task counts. BFS under F may already be cheaper in tokens on RW2. PlanBench's 3–5-block state spaces are also tiny: by my derivation from OEIS A000262, at most about 866 states for 5 blocks. The convincing amortization test is therefore on larger instances, such as the 6–20-block set where o1-preview drops to 23.6%.

== [1]/summary
(1) F-01 is a standard case of shortcut learning caused by underspecification. With training limits {32,48,64}, both "|2x| <= L" and a magnitude rule fit the training data perfectly, so the data cannot tell them apart (Geirhos 2020; D'Amour 2020/2022). Which feature a network picks then depends on how easy it is to extract (Hermann et al., ICLR 2024). Transformers trained on rule-governed data often learn such statistical features while staying near-perfect in distribution (Zhang et al. SimpleLogic; Liu et al. on automata shortcuts; Wu et al. counterfactual tasks; Meszaros et al. rule extrapolation, NeurIPS 2024). The closest world-model analogues are: Vafa et al. 2024 (96-99% valid taxi routes, but incoherent maps; validity falls to 0.08 with 10% detours); Vafa et al. 2025 (models learn task-specific heuristics instead of Newtonian law); Kang et al. 2024 (video world models are perfect in distribution but generalize case-by-case out of it); ByteSized32-SP (GPT-4 at most 59.9% on non-trivial transitions, failing mostly where arithmetic or common sense is needed); and Emami et al. 2026 (fine-tuned Blocksworld LLMs encode action validity linearly even when their output probabilities do not). What F-01 adds is a clean split inside one model: arithmetic extrapolates (99.5%) but the parameterized legality predicate does not (36.5%), and the data provably forces this. That fits Xu et al. (ICLR 2021): extrapolation works only when the needed nonlinearity is built into the architecture or features. (2) The idea behind F-03 — defer when your own confidence falls in a reject band — is old: Chow's reject option, selective classification, learning to defer, KWIK, and confidence-based LLM cascades (FrugalGPT, Jitkrittum 2023, Gupta 2024, AutoMix, KnowNo). Combining a fine-tuned model with its own pretrained model to recover out-of-distribution robustness is also well known (WiSE-FT, Kumar et al. calibrated ensembles, VRF sample-wise weighting); Nudging gates in the opposite direction, and Co-LLM learns token-level deferral. I found no paper that uses the LoRA model's own P(legal) to gate the same backbone with the adapter switched off as a yes/no verifier inside a learned world model for planning. But that absence comes from a non-exhaustive search, and the configuration is a specific instance of known principles. So the defensible novelty is empirical. The always-on base verifier is a weaker model in distribution (0.912) that is better on flips — the "specialist" case where Jitkrittum et al. say confidence deferral can fail — yet the band gate still works. Flips are an anticorrelated spurious feature, which is exactly the condition under which Kumar et al. (UAI 2022) say calibrated ensembles fail. And the soft-versus-confident asymmetry is a concrete case of Kamath et al. (ACL 2020): max-probability confidence is overconfident under shift. (3) Confident errors need a signal other than confidence, or new information. Options: input-novelty or hidden-state OOD scores (Ren et al. ICLR 2023, SIRC, a Kamath-style calibrator); hidden-state probes; methods that train deliberately different models to find alternative hypotheses (DivDis, D-BAT); counterfactual or parameter-randomized data (Kaushik et al. 2020); or a symbolic/program legality model (Thought of Search/AutoToS, WorldCoder, GIF-MCTS, WALL-E, SAM learning). Temperature scaling preserves ranking and cannot fix confident errors, and LoRA ensembles or Laplace-LoRA may not either, because every member shares the data-forced shortcut. Every result so far is single-seed; the flip accuracy of 0.949 on n=216 has a 95% CI of roughly +/-3 pts. Proposed priorities: R1, a baseline ladder with risk-coverage curves (novelty); R2, a gate based on novelty or probes (confident errors, no training); R3, a 2x2 retraining on parameter coverage x factorized output format; R4, an induced-program legality verifier as a ceiling baseline and a bridge to Blocksworld.

== [1]/recommendations[0]/idea
R1 (novelty of F-03): a deferral baseline ladder with risk-coverage curves, reported separately for ID, above-range flips and below-range keys. Baselines: (a) random deferral at the same ~18% verifier-call rate; (b) the current confidence band; (c) a Kumar-style calibrated ensemble (temperature-scale F's P(legal) and the base model's P(yes) on ID, then average); (d) a WiSE-FT analogue: LoRA scale alpha in {0.25, 0.5, 0.75} applied at inference; (e) proxy-tuning-style logit arithmetic between adapter-on and adapter-off; (f) a post-hoc learned deferral rule (logistic on [P(legal), |x|, L, op]) fit on calibration limits 80/112; (g) oracle deferral (defer only when F is wrong) as the upper bound. Report AURC, accuracy at matched verifier calls, and the fraction of oracle gain recovered, with bootstrap CIs over keys and 3 F seeds.

== [1]/recommendations[1]/idea
R2 (repair confident errors without training): add an epistemic, input-novelty signal to the gate. Step 0: measure the frozen base verifier's accuracy on the below-range confident-error keys (limit 21, doubling 11-14). If it is also wrong, no gate can help; go to R3/R4. Then compare gate signals at matched call rate: (a) the confidence band; (b) structural novelty: defer doubles whenever L lies outside the training-limit hull [32,64]; (c) relative Mahalanobis distance on F's final hidden state at the legality token, fit on ID (Ren et al. 2023); (d) a linear legality probe on adapter-on and adapter-off hidden states, trained on ID keys only; (e) a SIRC/Kamath-style combination of (a) with (c) or (d) via a small logistic deferral rule. Fit it on one below-range calibration limit (e.g. 24 or 26) plus 80/112, never on 21.

== [1]/recommendations[3]/why
Program world models generalize where neural ones do not: Thought of Search (GPT-4 code solves all 502 Blocksworld tasks after ~2.8 iterations), AutoToS (unit-test feedback, 100%), WorldCoder, GIF-MCTS and WALL-E. In RW2 the shortest consistent program should be |op(x)| <= L, because a limit-dependent rule explains all three training limits with no extra constant. That would repair both the soft and the confident side, matching the hand-written code-rule ceiling (0.954 / 0.995) while being learned (cf. the Solomonoff-style account in Meszaros et al. 2024). For Blocksworld, SAM learning recovers exact IPC action models from at most two trajectories, so reviewers will expect this baseline. Better to measure it now and frame the LM world model's value accordingly: amortization, noisy or textual states, or obfuscated domains where symbol grounding is the hard part.

== [4]/summary
Prior work does not treat "domain-trained small model plus search vs. zero-shot giant" as a fair head-to-head. The comparisons that hold up do three things. First, they report cost on several axes at once: params, training examples and FLOPs, LLM calls and tokens and FLOPs per instance, and dollars for API models. Snell et al. and Liu et al. add pretraining and inference FLOPs; Katz et al. count LLM calls; Kapoor et al. and Dehghani et al. ask for Pareto/cost-controlled reporting. Second, they put each system in a regime and compare like with like: zero-shot LLM, LRM, SFT specialist, verifier-in-loop (LLM-Modulo), or classical planner with the true model (Fast Downward is 100% at ~0.27 s/instance). Third, they show generalization the training data cannot explain: larger problems, obfuscated or random names, and freshly generated mirror sets.

The main criticism of your setup comes from Kambhampati: fine-tuning "is converting the planning task into a memory-based approximate retrieval... It doesn't prove that LLMs are able to plan." Huang et al. add that a fine-tuned planner tested on instances "generated using the same PDDL generator configurations as the training data" hides its failures. Their Qwen2-7B SFT dropped from 98.5% in-distribution to 13.5% on longer plans and 0% on obfuscated names. Bohnet et al. (96.3% to 34.2% on 8-20 blocks) and Emami et al. (59% at 6 blocks to 6% at 10) show the same collapse.

PlanBench's 600 instances are 3-5 blocks with optimal plans of at most 16 steps. They are also nearly saturated: R1 scores 99.1%, and your own leakage audit shows 54% of random 3-5-block problems are renamings of test items. So an in-distribution score, even a high one, is not news. The credible headline is size and name generalization at stated compute. PlanBench Blocksworld-Hard (110 instances, 6-20 blocks, 20-40 step plans) has published zero-shot scores to pair against: R1 53.6%, o1-preview 23.65%, o1-mini 10%. Back it up with randomized object names and Mystery Regime B, where o1-preview scores 52.8% (37.3% randomized) and R1 43.3% (25.8%).

Templates for a "small model plus search beats big model" claim: Snell et al.'s FLOPs-matched evaluation (X=6·N·D_pretrain vs Y=2·N·D_inference; beats a ~14x larger model on easy and medium questions but not hard ones); Wu et al. (Llemma-7B plus tree search beats 34B); Liu et al. (1B beats 405B at N=512; total FLOPs 100-1000x lower, but the paper does not say whether reward-model FLOPs were counted); Jones (each 10x of training compute saves about 15x of test-time compute in Hex); and in planning, RAP (LLaMA-33B plus MCTS with an LLM world model beats GPT-4 CoT) and Searchformer.

Minimum runs you must do yourself:
- Same-harness Qwen3-4B zero-shot, non-thinking and thinking at fixed token budgets, with both the NL and the JSON prompt.
- A larger open model (Qwen3-1.7B, or 4B with QLoRA if affordable) fine-tuned on exactly your data, plus stock Qwen3-0.6B with the same adapter recipe.
- Internal ablations at matched FLOPs: proposer only, F rerank, BFS under the learned F, and the exact-simulator ceiling.
- Hard-110 plus generated 7/8/10-block sets, a randomized-name rerun, and the fresh mirror set.
- Signature-clustered CIs (347 distinct problems) and at least 3 seeds.

Compare the published API systems only per instance, using dollars as their cost column, never as FLOP-matched opponents.

== [5]/summary
On the standard 600-instance PlanBench Blocksworld set (3-5 blocks: about 446 four-block and 55 five-block instances in generated_basic, plus 100 three-block instances), the only published fine-tuned result is GPT-3 davinci fine-tuned on 1,000 generated instances: 122/600 (~20%), with no search. Every other number on that exact set is a zero-shot or one-shot generalist. The best are DeepSeek-R1 at 595 (99.1%, from the repo leaderboard only) and o1-preview at 587. Every non-reasoning LLM scores ≤386, and none reaches 5% on Mystery Blocksworld. The small-model fine-tuning papers from 2022-2026 all use their own generated test sets, not the 600 IDs, and they agree that plain SFT nearly saturates in-distribution Blocksworld: Plansformer (CodeT5-base, ~220M) 90.0% valid; Qwen2-7B 98.5%; Gemma2-9B LoRA 99-100%; Llama-3-8B 99.3% at 5 blocks; Gemini 1.0 S 96.3%; PDDL-Instruct (Llama-3-8B with state-tracking CoT) 94%. SFT breaks on bigger problems and longer plans. Emami et al. (2026, Gemma2-9B LoRA trained on 3-5 blocks) fall to 53-59% on 6-block PlanBench instances and 0-6% at 10 blocks. Huang et al.'s Qwen2-7B gets 13.5% on 17-32-step plans (41.5% after RL) and 0% when symbols are obfuscated. Methods that pair an LLM with a world model or search either imagine with the LLM itself or use a sound symbolic model. RAP (LLaMA-33B as its own world model, MCTS, no environment feedback) averages ~64% on a 201-instance PlanBench split; LLM Reasoners reports 51% with Llama-2-70B. Thought of Search / AutoToS (LLM writes the successor function, then BFS) solve 100% of 502 instances with 2-10 LLM calls in total. LLM+P gets 90% on 20 IPC problems, and LLM-Modulo (GPT-4 with VAL feedback) 82% on a 50-instance subset. Tree-Planner (VirtualHome) and Searchformer (Sokoban/mazes) do not evaluate on Blocksworld; the nearest trained-search work on Blocksworld is System-1.x (Mistral-7B trained on A* traces), at 25-28% on 7-10-step plans. For harder cases there is the 110-instance PlanBench Blocksworld-hard set (6-20 blocks, 20-40-step plans): o1-preview 26/110, R1 53.6% (~59/110), o1-mini 10%, and o1-preview with a VAL loop 98.2%. On IPC-scale fresh tasks, Gemini 3.1 Pro now beats LAMA (35 vs 28 of 45 Blocksworld tasks). No single published comparator is fully fair. The fairest are: (a) for method, RAP on its own split, since it uses the same plan-in-imagination-under-a-model-plus-search regime; (b) for training regime, Emami et al. 2026 and Plansformer (LoRA or SFT on generated small Blocksworld, single greedy plan, VAL, tested on PlanBench or larger instances); (c) for the headline on the identical 600 IDs, fine-tuned GPT-3 (122) as the only fine-tuned entry, with R1/o1-preview as the ceiling. The single most important comparator is an internal one that the literature implies: a same-size, same-data plain-SFT plan generator without F. Because SFT saturates in-distribution, a score on the 600 set says nothing about F unless it is paired against that arm. On the 600 set, ≥570/600 is the expected level for a competent specialist. Matching 587 equals o1-preview and ≥590-595 ties R1, but this is news only with a significant paired gain over the no-F SFT arm or much lower compute. Anything below ~540 (90%) would trail a 220M Plansformer. A result that really rivals far larger models has to come from Blocksworld-hard without feedback: beating 26/110 beats o1-preview, beating ~59/110 beats R1, and ~108/110 would match o1-preview with a VAL loop, with no verifier.
